<div markdown="1" class="dropcap">
There is a question I am asked constantly and never ask myself. It arrives from every direction at once: from the feeds, where a man in a rented lens explains weekly how his hobby became recurring revenue; from the AI corner of the internet, which has decided that the technology's highest purpose is a funnel; from people I like, who look at something I built and gently ask whether I have thought about monetizing it. Nobody asks whether I want to sell. The wanting is assumed, the way weather is assumed. This post is the answer I do not give in the room.
</div>

## I. The Pitch
{: #the-pitch}

The pressure has a grammar, and the grammar is worth inspecting before I refuse it. A tool is a product opportunity. A repository is validated demand. A post about something you built is content, and the only question content can raise is what it funnels toward. The AI gold rush did not invent this grammar; it pressurized it. Every new capability is followed within the week by an industry explaining how to wrap it, price it, and scale it, and behind that industry a second one, selling courses about the first.

I want to be precise about what I am refusing, because it is almost none of it. The previous post on this site documents five months of reverse engineering executed by AI agent fleets, four at a time, every claim cited to a file and line. I run the gold rush's own tools all day. I am in the river up to my elbows. What I have noticed is that the people reliably making money in a gold rush are selling maps, and that selling maps changes what a person is able to see when they look at the territory. Underneath all of it sits a single assumption, that the point of a tool is to become a price, and that assumption is the pressure.

## II. The Refusal
{: #the-refusal}

So, flatly and without apology: I have zero interest in profit. That sentence gets read as modesty, or as a phase a first real invoice will cure, so let me extend it. I am never going to be a salesman. Salesmanship is not a channel you bolt onto the work; it is a redesign of the worker. The moment a thing must sell, the curiosity starts deferring to the buyer: features chosen for the demo, corners softened for the market's comfort, the roadmap written by whoever is most likely to pay next quarter. I build opinionated tools for an opinionated library, and the entire point of each one is that it exists because it interested me, not because a segment was waiting for it. Monetize that and you have not found the workshop a business model; you have closed a workshop and opened a factory where it stood.

I am thirty-nine. I am a former chef, a computer engineering student, the curator of a four-thousand-six-hundred-book library, and the proprietor of the workshop in question, whose roadmap has always been whatever interested me that week. I have exactly one career plan. I just want to build cool shit.

## III. The Case
{: #the-case}

Because abstraction is cheap, here is the live decision. [bindery-cli](/codex/bindery/) is a command-line surgeon for malformed EPUBs: it repairs broken books by the thousand, dry-run first and guarded by safety nets, and its opt-in modes strip the garbage publishers bake in, stale pagination, distributor watermarks, illegal markup. It exists because my own library needed it. The idea currently on the table is a browser version, and the interesting part is the shape of the idea: no accounts, no advertisements, no tiers, no watermark on page one reading *upgrade to pro*. A visitor arrives with a broken file and leaves with a whole one, and nothing in the exchange has a price. Whether it would carry a donation link is genuinely undecided. Donations are the one revenue shape that does not redesign the product; there is nothing to gate and nobody to please, and even that link sits wrong on the day I try to imagine it, so it stays a question rather than a plan.

And here is the part the monetization chorus cannot parse: the hard problem is not how to charge for such a thing. The hard problem is how to host it at all. bindery's day job includes stripping watermarks out of other people's books, so a hosted version means copyrighted bytes moving through a server with my name on it, and the engineering answer is probably that the whole tool runs in the visitor's browser, every repair happening on their machine, no book ever touching a server of mine. Hold that shape for a second. To give a tool away safely, I have to design it so I cannot see what it is used on. I have received a great deal of advice about the question I am not asking, and none whatsoever about the one I am.

## IV. The Arithmetic of Enough
{: #the-arithmetic-of-enough}

I scoff at six-figure programming jobs, and scoffing owes an argument, so here is mine. I keep double-entry books in plaintext, hledger, which means I know what my life costs to the dollar. In the small town where I live, the number is a quiet fifty thousand a year. That is the entire ambition, stated in full: a wage that covers the arithmetic and a workshop nobody invoices.

The gap between fifty and a hundred and fifty is denominated in what the paying jobs attach to the money: a roadmap written to someone else's quarter, standups at someone else's morning, a career pointed at someone else's product. Some people want that trade, and I hold nothing against them. The delta above enough is priced in evenings and attention, in a curiosity allowed to pick its own targets, which is the only wealth I have ever been ambitious about. I spent years in kitchens, a trade that monetizes passion by default and calls the result hospitality, and I watched what selling a craft does to the person carrying it. I liked the craft too much to load it onto a truck.

What I cannot tell you is how the living actually arrives. That part is honest uncertainty: a technician diploma, a bachelor's still to come, an entry-level market not exactly handing out its legendary salaries these days. What I know is the shape the work has to have. Enough, not maximal. Local, not coastal. Quiet, not loud. A salary that buys the hours back at the end of each day, because the hours are the workshop's raw material and everything I have ever built came out of them.

<p class="ornament ornament--asterism">⁂</p>

The masthead at the top of this site reads Vir Invictus, the unconquered, and the motto has started doing work I did not entirely plan for it. The unconquered is not the richest man in the room. He is the one the market did not buy, because nothing he had was for sale. The workshop stays open, the tools stay free, the books stay whole. I want a quiet fifty thousand, a small town, low overhead, and the freedom to build whatever interests me.

Is that too much to ask?
